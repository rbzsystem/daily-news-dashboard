The readThe best first quarter the Motion Picture Group has ever had, and it was one movie Revenue of $776.6M was up 48% and Motion Picture revenue more than doubled to $587.3M, but the number that matters is segment profit: $105.0M against $2.4M a year ago, a Q1 record. That is Michael — a billion-dollar worldwide gross and, per the release, the highest-grossing biopic of all time — plus the ancillary tail on The Housemaid. Adjusted OIBDA swung to $79.3M from negative $3.7M. Strip out the swing and the underlying company did not change much this quarter; one title did.
· Lionsgate Studios (NYSE: LION) · FQ1 FY2027 (qtr ended 6/30/26; FY ends 3/31, so the Jun quarter is FQ1 of FY27) · reported 8/6/26 after market close (call 5:00 PM ET) · shares opened 8/7 at $13.34, +3.2% vs. the 8/6 close of $12.93, and gave most of it back by midday (~$13.06, +1.0%)
The balance sheet moved faster than the P&L, and that is the real news
Leverage improved nearly two full turns from the March quarter to 4.3x, hitting the mid-4x target Barge had guided to earlier than he expected. Adjusted free cash flow was $128.9M against negative $111.9M a year ago. Barge now guides to 3–3.5x in FY28 and below 3x after, with the 3 Arts put worth about half a turn as a temporary bump. A de-levering Lionsgate is one with room to spend on IP and talent rather than one negotiating from defense — the same read as last quarter, now materially further along.

Television was the soft leg, and management is asking for two more quarters
TV revenue fell to $189.3M from $288.5M and segment profit to $10.2M from $26.0M, entirely on delivery timing. The company is holding its "double scripted deliveries in FY27" outlook and says segment profit improves sequentially in Q2 then accelerates in the back half. That is an unfalsifiable claim until Q3. Watch it: the $1.5B backlog (+21%) is the collateral behind the promise.

The arms-dealer thesis got its proof point — Power to Netflix
Lionsgate licensed the first four Power series to Netflix — all four internationally, the original worldwide — for three years starting in November. Packer deliberately kept it to three years to preserve the next cycle, and flagged Orange Is the New Black, Mythic Quest and Mad Men (post-HBO) returning to distribution. Feltheimer's framing: "Streamers need a lot of content, we have a lot of content." The library is $987M TTM, and the single biggest contributor this quarter was a 38-year-old title, Dirty Dancing.
Operational Notes
  • Real greenlight appetite, and it is castable now. Management claims more than a dozen branded repeatable properties over three years: four wrapped (Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, John Rambo with Noah Centineo, Caine with Donnie Yen directing and starring, plus the Gibson Resurrection two-parter in campaign), three about to start. The Housemaid's Secrets adds Brittany Snow to Sydney Sweeney/Kirsten Dunst/Paul Anthony Kelly, production October for a December 2027 release. Blair Witch (Blumhouse + Atomic Monster) is going to rising director Dylan Clark — flagged on the call as a 3 Arts client. `Naruto` is "out to cast" with Destin Daniel Cretton directing, described as a major tentpole. That is a live packaging window.
  • 3 Arts is the item to read twice. The strategic review is ongoing and Weinstein was explicit that the transactions under consideration are "not ones that are purely financial" — strategic elements attached. The business is pushing into sports and creator: Travis Kelce, Myles Garrett, Taylor Rooks, plus new clients Colston Loveland and Sophie Cunningham. Roughly 30 shared projects with Lionsgate. He cited the THE•TEAM/Providence Equity transaction as proof these are "scarce businesses with premium multiples." Direct comp intelligence for anyone valuing a management/representation asset.
  • TV is selling through non-traditional structures, not just to more buyers. 13-for-13 on scripted renewals across 12 different buyers. Two pickups this week: DINKS (Marta Kauffman) at Amazon as a fully sponsor-funded series with Publicis Groupe, and Trauma (Richard Madden) starting with Prime Video UK then Paramount+ for the U.S. and rest of world. Beggs: the "secret sauce" is getting shows made in ways that are not how shows are normally sold. Expect more split-territory and brand-funded structures in Lionsgate deals.
  • AI posture is now aggressive and operational, with a talent-likeness edge. 95% workforce adoption, a Runway deal, and Feltheimer volunteered a live use case: generating video versions of audio-only podcasts for a piece of talent 3 Arts manages. He framed it as "we always see these as tools that enhance the work that we do with talent," but that specific application is derivative content built on a managed client's material. Worth tracking for consent/likeness/compensation terms.
  • On Paramount–WBD, Lionsgate wants the deal done. With the transaction paused and trial not until March, Feltheimer said "uncertainty is the worst thing for our business," praised David Ellison, and said a better-financed competitive streamer is better for Lionsgate on both original programming and library sales — noting they have already sold Ellison's side a new TV show and are discussing co-financing features. Fogelson added they had not been doing much with Paramount or HBO and is "already seeing signs" of that changing.
  • `Michael` economics, on the record. Fogelson: "nothing particularly unusual" — the estate and filmmakers take an appropriate profit participation; Universal shared financial responsibility and upside internationally except Japan (Kino). Profitability modeled over roughly a 10-year cycle, front-loaded, and it was a top PVOD performer. Sequel: production targeted end of calendar 2026 into early 2027, release somewhere between end of calendar 2027 and 1H 2028, with previously shot musical sequences to be reused. No budget guidance yet.
  • Macro the company is leaning on: domestic box office heading for its first $10B year since before the pandemic, and Lionsgate claiming north of 10% domestic share for the first half of the calendar year.

The readThe headline number — net income down 91% to $149M — is a base-effect illusion, not an operating collapse: Q2 2025 booked a one-time $2.958B gain on debt extinguishment that Q2 2026 didn't repeat (this year books a $75M loss on the same line), a nearly $3B swing on one non-operating item that alone explains most of the year-over-year drop. Studios cratered 89% ex-FX to $96M on a brutal comp (Q2'25 had A Minecraft Movie, Sinners, and Final Destination Bloodlines) and management's own admission that "a handful of recent films... underperformed expectations." For anyone negotiating with this buyer, the two disclosures that matter more than either headline are the first concrete production-cadence number WBD has ever given (14 films this year, ramping to 19 in 2027) and a new hard outside date on the pending Paramount Skydance sale — the closing is now on hold until five days after litigation resolves, or June 1, 2027 — that management still won't take questions on.

Key Takeaways
The 91% net income "plunge" is a one-time comp; the real story is a Studios air pocket against Streaming's best quarter ever
Net income available to WBD fell to $149M from $1.58B [R] — but the release states $1.1B of that swing is pre-tax acquisition-related amortization, content fair-value step-up, and restructuring [R], and the single largest driver is non-operating: a $2.958B gain on debt extinguishment in Q2 2025 versus a $75M loss in Q2 2026 [R], a ~$3B one-line swing. Adjusted EBITDA — the cleaner operating read — fell a more modest 6% ex-FX to $1.879B [R]. Inside that: Studios Adjusted EBITDA collapsed 89% ex-FX to $96M from $863M [R], driven by a weak theatrical slate against an exceptional 2025 comp and, on the call, Wiedenfels acknowledging "the quarter, obviously, in the film business, wasn't what we expected" [call]. He stated "zero doubts about our long-term $3 billion EBITDA target for the studio" [call] and gave WBD's first-ever disclosed production-cadence number: 14 films this year, ramping to 19 in 2027 [call] — a concrete, trackable output figure worth pricing into any studio pitch or slate conversation with this buyer. Zaslav separately clarified the 2026 slate ran deliberately light on tentpoles ("we're a little light on those tentpoles" this year) with 2027 loaded instead (Lord of the Rings, Batman, Superman, a second Minecraft) [call] — a scheduling choice, not a capacity retreat.

Streaming crossed $3B in quarterly revenue for the first time — but WBD stopped disclosing subscriber counts and ARPU this quarter
Streaming revenue hit $3.079B (+10% ex-FX) [R], Adjusted EBITDA $512M (+63% ex-FX, ~17% margin) [R], both records; subscriber-related revenue growth accelerated 200bps sequentially to 10% ex-FX [R]. Management reiterated its long-term 20%+ Streaming EBITDA margin target [R, shareholder letter]. Notably absent: this quarter's release, shareholder letter, and trending schedule contain no total subscriber count and no ARPU figure anywhere [R, by omission] — a break from the format used through at least Q3 2025 (domestic/international subscriber and ARPU breakdown) and even Q1 2026 (which disclosed HBO Max surpassing 140M subscribers, per the prior-quarter transcript summary). The letter instead leads with ad-tier mix (~40% of global HBO Max subscribers on the ad-supported tier, +11pp YoY [R]) and subscriber-related revenue growth. Whether this is a one-quarter omission or a deliberate framing shift toward revenue-per-user over raw scale is worth watching next quarter — for now, anyone valuing HBO Max scale in a negotiation has one fewer hard number to anchor on.

The Paramount close now carries an explicit outside date tied to litigation, and management still won't discuss it
The shareholder letter states plainly: "the closing of the transaction is on hold until the earlier of five days after legal proceedings are complete or June 1, 2027" [R] — the first hard backstop date WBD has put on paper (Paramount's own Q2 materials, filed two days earlier, disclosed the underlying antitrust trial date as March 2027). Management took no questions on the transaction per stated policy, and when Rich Greenfield asked directly what a company split would look like in a scenario where the deal doesn't happen, Zaslav did not answer the question — he repeated confidence the deal will close [call]. Net leverage sits at 3.4x on $33.1B gross debt and $3.4B cash [R]; during the quarter WBD refinanced the remaining $15B bridge facility into a $13B + €1.7B Term Loan B that drew ~$40B of investor demand and is expected to save ~150bps of annual interest cost [R] — balance-sheet execution that reads as WBD running the standalone business at full capability regardless of deal timing, the same "operate from strength" posture Paramount struck in its own 8/4 report.

IMAX 70mm is a hard-capped scarce asset, and Gelfond said plainly he will not expand it much
41 IMAX film locations worldwide did $6.3M in The Odyssey's opening weekend, roughly $153,000 per screen [call]. Asked directly whether the 70mm network could be doubled, Gelfond said no in economic terms: the projector alone is $50,000, the booth needs a forklift, the aspect ratio demands an oversized house, and "there are a finite number of locations that really can support the economics" [call]. He also disclosed that IMAX subsidizes the film prints and "get[s] a significantly higher take rate in those film theaters than we get in regular theaters" [call]. Two things follow for anyone representing a filmmaker. First, a 70mm commitment is a genuinely scarce term, not a marketing courtesy, and it should be negotiated as one — count of locations, run length, and whether the picture holds those houses when the next IMAX title arrives. Second, IMAX's own economics are best when a film plays long in those houses, which aligns IMAX with a filmmaker arguing for a protected theatrical run and against a distributor arguing for velocity.

IMAX is now openly lobbying exhibitors to raise ticket prices on film engagements
Asked whether he would re-cut the revenue share with studios or exhibitors on the back of The Odyssey, Gelfond declined: "I think there's no reason to snatch defeat from the hands of victory... We're not gonna be pigs about it" [call]. He then named the one place he does want movement: price. "With tickets at Lincoln Square being scalped for $1,000, I think there's room to move up the prices for a film. We've let that be known to the exhibitors in the film area, but it's their decision" [call]. This is a company telling the market it believes premium theatrical is underpriced. For a client with box-office-linked backend, that is a live variable in the next 12 months, and it argues for defining bonus and escalator thresholds against gross rather than admissions.

The Nolan playbook is now a template IMAX is actively selling to other filmmakers
IMAX redesigned its legacy film camera from scratch, retaining only the original mechanical movement, and built "the most advanced camera sound blimp ever created" so Nolan could shoot sync sound in IMAX for the first time [call]. Nolan shot 2.1 million feet of film. Gelfond then described a formalized program: "we have a plan and a presentation already put together about what The Odyssey meant to this movie and why they should film going forward" [call], with named targets — Greta Gerwig on Narnia, Michael B. Jordan on The Thomas Crown Affair, Denis Villeneuve on Dune: Part Three, and the Ryan Coogler Sinners precedent held up as the model of a director "leaning in." The read for talent representation: bespoke technical investment and co-marketing are on the table for filmmakers with leverage, and IMAX measures the payoff in box office it can attribute to the filmmaker's participation. That is a negotiable, provable contribution — and it belongs in the marketing-commitment and credit conversations, not just the format conversation.

Two exclusive-window precedents are sitting in the slate, and both are worth watching
Netflix's Fincher-directed, Pitt-starring Cliff Booth project is going out as an "IMAX-exclusive theatrical release" [call], and Gerwig's Narnia "opens exclusively in IMAX before its wide global release" [call]. A streamer buying a premium-format-only theatrical window, and a major studio using IMAX as a pre-wide exclusive tier, are the same structural idea: the format is becoming a distinct distribution window with its own economics. If that holds, "theatrical release" stops being a binary in a term sheet and becomes a ladder — IMAX-exclusive, premium-wide, wide — each rung with a different guarantee, run length, and backend trigger.

Key Takeaways
The separation is now an execution timeline, not a proposal — roughly one year out, with the buyback paused to fund it

What this means practically for the next twelve months: NBCU is a buyer operating under a corporate reorganization, which historically produces slower decisions, duplicated mandates during transition, and executives whose reporting lines are about to change. Deals that need a signature from a level that is being restructured should be pushed to close early rather than allowed to drift into the separation window.

Cavanagh's answer on scale is the most useful thing on this call for a seller
Asked by Jessica Reif Ehrlich whether NBCU has enough scale to compete alone or whether the spin is really about creating consolidation currency, Cavanagh took both halves and then added a third: an independent NBCU will compete by being the partner-friendly one. "Other strategies are a little more walled gardens. Our approach is to build great businesses that serve our own platforms, but look for opportunities to partner, bundle, and exhibit other people's IP in our parks and create IP in our studios that go to other platforms" [call]. That is a stated posture on third-party licensing, cross-platform windowing, and IP that does not have to stay in-house. For a producer or filmmaker weighing where a project can live and travel, NBCU is signaling it will let content out — the opposite of the Disney/Netflix retention logic. It is also, read cynically, exactly what a company positions itself as when it wants to be an attractive merger partner. Both readings are consistent with the words.

Peacock's first profitable quarter is real, but it flattered a Media segment that got worse underneath
Peacock generated $189M of EBITDA on $1.9B of revenue, against a $101M loss on $1.2B a year ago — a $290M swing [R] — with paid subscribers up 2M sequentially to 48M and up 7M year over year [R]. Peacock revenue rose 54%, distribution over 50%, advertising nearly 70% [call]. But Media segment EBITDA rose only 3.7% to $708M on revenue up 25.3% [R], because operating expenses rose 29.1% [R] on NBA rights and World Cup programming. Back out Peacock and the rest of Media — NBC, Telemundo, Bravo, international networks — generated roughly $519M of EBITDA this quarter against roughly $784M a year ago, a decline of about a third (computed from the segment disclosure; Comcast does not present it this way, so treat it as our inference, not a reported figure). Cavanagh's own framing is that this is deliberate: the objective was never "Peacock profitability unto itself" but the integrated Media result [call]. Fair. The consequence for talent is the same either way: the linear side is funding the streaming turn, and cost discipline on the linear side is where it comes from.

Studios had a genuinely strong quarter, and the composition is the point

Universal is currently winning with a genuinely mixed portfolio — franchise animation, a specialty label breaking its own record, an original from a legacy filmmaker, and an auteur event picture. That is the profile of a buyer with appetite across categories rather than one retreating into IP.

Key Takeaways
Record EBITDA on structurally lower attendance — the exhibitor's cost base, not the box office, is what changed
Revenue $1,596.7M [R] (+14.2%) and Adjusted EBITDA $321.4M [R] (+69.6%) are both all-time company records, and the first quarter above $300M of EBITDA ever. Margin went 13.6% → 20.1% [R]. The frame that actually matters came from CFO Sean Goodman: against Q2 2019, with the North American box office ~7.5% smaller and AMC's own attendance ~26.5% lower across ~16% fewer locations, AMC generated 6% more revenue and 39.5% more Adjusted EBITDA [call]. Roughly $200M of incremental revenue converted to $131.9M of incremental EBITDA — about 66% flow-through [call]. AMC does not need the box office to recover to 2019 to earn 2019 economics. That is the structural point, and it is now demonstrated rather than asserted.

Breakeven is ~$10.4B domestic — the exhibitor's solvency objection to theatrical windows is losing force
Pressed by Stonex on what box office level sustains positive free cash flow on a rolling 12-month basis, Aron gave a specific number: "right around $10.4 billion" [call], and said further leverage-driven interest reduction pushes it lower. Q2 free cash flow was $190.1M [R]; the first half was +$15.4M [R] against -$328.1M a year ago. AMC ended the quarter with $778.4M cash [R] and expects no material debt maturities before 2029 [R]. Read it into a deal: the largest exhibitor in the world is no longer negotiating from distress. Arguments that shortened windows or day-and-date are survivable "because exhibition needs the content anyway" get weaker as AMC's ability to hold a position gets stronger. For filmmakers and producers who want a real theatrical run, the counterparty who benefits from that has money again.

Premium format is now the whole economic story — 8% of screens delivered more than half of The Odyssey's gross
AMC and Odeon operate ~750 premium or enhanced auditoriums: 226 IMAX, 182 Dolby Cinema, 83 iSense, 47 PRIME, 14 ScreenX/4DX and 193 XL [call]. Those are roughly 8% of the circuit's screens and produced more than 50% of AMC's ticket gross on The Odyssey [call]. IMAX and Dolby carry a $6–7 price premium; XL carries ~10% [call]. XL conversions cost under $20,000 a screen because the screens already existed [call], and AMC intends to add 100–250 more PLF/XL auditoriums over two to four years, "mostly using third-party capital" [call]. The talent-side translation is direct: format availability, not screen count, is increasingly what determines a film's opening. "Shot for IMAX" is a measurable revenue lever, and premium-screen commitments and format-exclusivity windows belong in the distribution conversation rather than being treated as marketing color.

Merchandise alone should exceed $100M globally in 2026, from zero four years ago [call]. This is a better quality of growth than price hikes, but it is levered to the slate supplying format-worthy, merchandisable titles. A quarter with six $75M+ domestic openings supplied that; a thin quarter would not.

Key Takeaways
An in-line quarter sold off on cash flow, not operations
Revenue $12.56B [R] (+13% Y/Y, +12% F/X-neutral) and operating margin 33.4% [R] both landed where Netflix said they would, and EPS of $0.80 [R] edged consensus of $0.79 [2nd]. The damage was free cash flow: $1.53B [R], down 33% from $2.27B, against roughly $2.7B expected [2nd]. Management attributes it to higher cash tax payments driven in part by the $2.8B Warner Bros. termination fee — the break fee Netflix collected in February when WBD walked from its Netflix merger agreement to take Paramount Skydance's offer, paid by Paramount [2nd]. Netflix reaffirmed ~$12.5B FCF for the year [R], so this reads as tax timing on a windfall rather than operating deterioration. Worth naming plainly: the FCF hole is the cost of the $2.8B, not evidence the business softened. Revenue growth genuinely is decelerating though (13.4% this quarter, 11.7% guided for Q3 [R]), and that is the real thing the market is repricing.

Netflix is dimming the engagement lights just as engagement became the industry's currency
After today's report, the semiannual What We Watched moves to annual, starting 2027 [R]. Netflix's stated reason is to keep focus on revenue and operating profit, its "primary financial metrics." Weekly Top 10s and title-level view hours survive. Read it straight: the richest public audit surface for how a title actually performed will refresh once a year instead of twice. For anyone negotiating performance-based compensation, bonus triggers, or renewal leverage against Netflix viewership, the independent verification window just got materially narrower — and it narrowed in the same quarter management declined, on the record, to detail its internal "quality" metric because it is "a competitive advantage." Greg Peters was pressed directly on softening hours-per-member and answered by reframing engagement as quality/variety/quantity rather than hours. That reframe is doing real work: it decouples the number Netflix reports from the number that determines value, at exactly the moment the reported number slows.

GenAI became the production standard — and the talent-compensation half of the question went unanswered
GenAI workflows ran through roughly 300 Netflix titles in 2026 [R], concentrated in post. The documentary series American Experiment carries 17 minutes of AI-enhanced footage produced "twice as fast and at half the cost" [call]. Netflix now runs InterPositive (acquired — Q1'26 cash flow shows $585.7M of acquisitions [R]) alongside Eyeline and its animation lab. The tell is in the Q&A: Morgan Stanley asked whether savings land in more content or better compensating talent. Sarandos answered "reinvested into more content on the service" and did not engage the compensation half at all. That is an inference, not a disclosure — but it is the kind of silence worth pricing into an AI-provisions negotiation. His framing ("movies are being made by people who make movies") is the reassurance; the capital allocation answer is the position.

Builders not buyers — $2.8B banked, $4.7B returned, and a flat denial on Lionsgate
Netflix repurchased $4.71B of stock [R], its largest buyback quarter ever, with ~$27.1B of authorization left [R]. Asked about Lionsgate reports (which Netflix has denied) and NBCU speculation, Sarandos repeated "primarily builders, not buyers" and a "very high bar" for large M&A; Neumann added there is "no change to our capital allocation philosophy." The $2.8B walk-away, followed immediately by the biggest buyback in company history, is the most credible signal available that Netflix means it. For sellers, this closes off the hope that Netflix becomes an acquirer of scaled libraries in the near term. The buying continues through licensing, partnership, and production, not the balance sheet.
Operational Notes
  • Live economics are now explicit, and they are a talent argument: live is ~5% of 2026 content spend but only ~1% of view hours — yet six of the top 10 new member sign-up days over five years are live events [R]. Netflix directly compared it to animation/kids TV at the same ~5% spend generating ~8% of view hours [call], and said it values both equally. That is Netflix stating on the record that it will pay for acquisition impact, not hours. Anyone whose deal is priced on view hours should note that Netflix itself argues hours are the wrong unit.
  • Netflix as distributor of other people's services: the TF1 integration in France (four weeks in) folds a local broadcaster's linear channels and on-demand library into the Netflix subscription at no extra cost; a TF1 title has already hit the French Top 10 [R]. Peters would not commit to more but said Netflix will "certainly consider" similar deals. Asked about bundling with Peacock or building a channel store, he neither ruled it out nor advanced it. This is the quarter's most under-priced structural signal: Netflix is testing whether it becomes the shelf other services sit on.
  • Creator and IP inflow keeps widening: video podcasts (Jay Shetty's On Purpose, Kate/Oliver Hudson, Martha Stewart via iHeartMedia), creators (Ms. Rachel — 27 weeks in the Global Top 10; Danny Go!; Salish & Jordan Matter; Hot Ones' Sean Evans), and publisher deals with Condé Nast, Hearst and People launching in August [R]. Podcasts over-index on daytime and mobile, which Netflix reads as incremental engagement [R]. New buyer behavior for non-traditional clients; also new competition for the same attention Netflix pays scripted talent for.
  • A FAST tier is considered but not coming: Peters said a free offering "could make sense in some markets" but flagged cannibalization and said there are "no near-term plans" [call]. Free trials are being re-tested for non-rejoining new members outside the US and UK [R].
  • Games are past the signal stage: cloud game monthly active players up 11x since October; FIFA World Cup: Launch Edition and Unhinged were the two biggest cloud debuts; Netflix Playground daily players up 3x, kids mobile game engagement up 600% Y/Y [call]. Investment stays "very small relative to overall content spend."
  • Ads on track to roughly double to ~$3B in 2026 [R]; US upfront commitments expected to close within weeks [R]. Programmatic access extends to Pause Ads and live inventory this summer.
  • Content spend growth is disciplined, not accelerating: content expense up ~10% in 2026 — above the 8% five-year average, below the 14% ten-year average [call]. Content amortization also ~+10% for 2026, front-half weighted, which is why Q2 operating income grew slower than revenue [R].
  • Awards posture intact: Emmy nominations "in nearly every category" this week [call]. Prestige still earns its slot in the mix.
  • Price increases are landing: first-half changes in the US, Mexico and Spain performed consistent with prior increases [R]; UCAN's 10% growth reflects only a partial-quarter benefit [R].
